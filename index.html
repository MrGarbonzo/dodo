Meet the Dodo

The dodo was a large, flightless bird that lived only on the island of Mauritius in the Indian Ocean, east of Madagascar. With no natural predators on the island, it had no need to fly. Within a century of humans arriving, it was gone, and it became the world's most famous symbol of extinction.

Fascinating Facts

A giant pigeon

Its closest living relative is the colorful Nicobar pigeon. The dodo's ancestors flew to Mauritius and gradually lost the ability to fly.

A cousin went extinct too

The Rodrigues solitaire, a close relative from the nearby island of Rodrigues, also died out, by the late 1700s.

Stone swallowers

Dodos swallowed stones (gastroliths) to help grind tough food like fruits, nuts and seeds in their gizzards.

A hooked beak

Its large, hooked beak was up to about 23 cm (9 in) long and was its most recognizable feature.

Mystery name

The name may come from the Dutch "dodoor" (sluggard) or "dodaars," or from the Portuguese "doudo," meaning fool.

Ground nester

Dodos are thought to have laid a single egg in a nest on the ground, making it easy prey for animals humans brought along.

Alice in Wonderland

Lewis Carroll put a Dodo in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), possibly a nod to his own stammer, "Do-do-Dodgson."

Rare remains

The only soft tissue left from a dodo is a head and foot at Oxford University Museum of Natural History. Most skeletons come from the Mare aux Songes swamp in Mauritius.

National symbol

The dodo appears on the coat of arms of Mauritius and is the country's national symbol.

Timeline

Before 1598
Dodos live undisturbed on Mauritius, with no land mammals hunting them.
1598
Dutch sailors land on Mauritius and record the dodo for the first time.
1600s
Hunting, plus rats, pigs, monkeys and other animals brought by ships, devastate dodos and their eggs. Forests are cleared.
1662
The last widely accepted sighting of a dodo, on an islet off Mauritius.
~1690
Some researchers estimate the true extinction happened around this time.
1865
Bones are excavated from Mare aux Songes, sparking new scientific interest.
Today
Scientists keep studying dodo DNA and bones, and some companies are even exploring "de-extinction."

Myth vs. Truth

Myth: Dodos were fat and clumsy

Old paintings often showed overfed captive birds or were guesswork.

Truth: They were likely leaner and quicker

Studies of bones suggest wild dodos were slimmer and reasonably agile, well suited to their forest home.

Myth: Dodos were stupid

"Dumb as a dodo" stuck because they didn't run from people.

Truth: They simply had no reason to fear

Having evolved without predators, they were naive, not dim. A 2016 brain study suggests their intelligence was similar to pigeons'.
